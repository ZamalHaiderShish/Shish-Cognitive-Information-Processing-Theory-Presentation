Good evening, everyone. My name is Zamal Haider Shish. Tonight I'm presenting Cognitive Information Processing theory, or CIP for short. CIP is a theory about how our minds take in information, store it, and pull it back out when we need it. By the end, I hope you'll see your own memory a little differently. Let's start with a short story.

What is Cognitive Information Processing?

Three core assumptions

Stages

Information moves from sensory to working to long-term memory (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968).

Limits

Each stage holds only so much, so not everything gets through.

Interaction

What we already know shapes what we notice.

Its roots

Objectivism

Knowledge exists outside the learner (Driscoll, 2004).

Let's start with the big picture. In the 1950s and 60s, many psychologists studied only behavior, meaning what people do. CIP asked a new question: what happens inside the mind? [CLICK: the computer appears] To answer it, researchers used the computer as a model. As Driscoll puts it, stimuli became inputs, and behavior became outputs. In between is processing, and that's where learning happens. Atkinson and Shiffrin described this processing as three memory stores. Information enters sensory memory, then moves to working memory. If we process it well, it's stored in long-term memory. [CLICK: the core assumptions appear] CIP rests on three core assumptions. First, information moves through these stages, step by step. Second, each stage has limits, so not everything gets through. Third, the system is interactive. What we already know shapes what we notice and how we understand it. Driscoll also links CIP to objectivism. That means knowledge exists out in the world, and the learner takes it in and processes it. Now let's follow information on its journey, starting with how it gets in.

Getting information in

  1. Sensory memory

    Takes in almost everything we see and hear, but lasts only 1 to 3 seconds.

  2. Attention

    A spotlight that selects a small part. It is limited, but not all-or-nothing (Driscoll, 2004).

  3. Automaticity

    Practiced skills, like typing or driving, need little attention.

  4. Pattern recognition and perception

    We notice key features and match them to what we already know.

(Title only.) Next, let's look at how information gets in. [CLICK: the pond and sensory memory] Information first enters sensory memory. Look at this pond. Your eyes take in all of it at once: the trees, the water, the reeds. Sensory memory holds almost everything, but only for one to three seconds. Then most of it fades. [CLICK: attention] What saves information is attention. Think of attention as a spotlight. It picks a small part of what our senses take in, like the water lily and the butterfly, and the rest is lost. Driscoll notes that attention is limited, but it isn't all-or-nothing. Have you ever heard your name across a noisy room? That's attention catching something that matters to you. [CLICK: automaticity] Attention can also become automatic. When you first learned to drive, it was hard to talk at the same time. Now you can, because practice made driving automatic. That frees up attention for other things. [CLICK: pattern recognition] Next comes pattern recognition, or perception. Our minds notice key features and match them to what we already know. That's why you can read messy handwriting. You match the shapes to letters you've seen before. Once we've noticed and recognized something, it moves into working memory.

Storing and using information

Working memory

Our mental workspace. It holds about 7 ± 2 chunks (Miller, 1956) for 20 to 30 seconds.

Encoding

Moving information into long-term memory by linking it to what we know, organizing it into chunks, and making mental pictures (words plus pictures are called dual coding).

Long-term memory

Almost unlimited and long-lasting. Knowledge is organized in schemas, which are networks of connected ideas.

Retrieval and forgetting

Retrieval means pulling information back out. We forget because of failure to encode, retrieval failure, decay, or interference (Driscoll, 2004).

Working memory is our mental workspace. It's where we think. But it's small. Miller found that it holds about seven items, plus or minus two. And it keeps them for only about 20 to 30 seconds. Look at him in the gallery: he can hold only a few pieces of the Mona Lisa in mind at once. Think of someone telling you a phone number. You repeat it until you dial, and then it's gone. Repeating like that is called rehearsal. It keeps information alive in working memory, but it doesn't store it well. [CLICK: encoding] To store information, we need encoding. Encoding works best when we link new information to what we already know, organize it into chunks, or picture it in our mind. Using words and pictures together is called dual coding. Here he's putting the painting together piece by piece: the eyes, the smile, the hands. [CLICK: long-term memory] Encoded information goes into long-term memory. Long-term memory is almost unlimited, and it can last a lifetime. It's organized in schemas, which are networks of connected ideas. Now the whole painting is mapped out in his mind, and every part connects to the others. [CLICK: retrieval and forgetting] The last step is retrieval, which means pulling information back out when we need it. Here he paints the Mona Lisa from memory. When retrieval fails, we forget. Driscoll names four causes. One: we never encoded it, like forgetting where you parked because you weren't paying attention. Two: it's stored, but we can't find it, like a word on the tip of your tongue. Three: decay, when a memory fades over time. Four: interference, when other information gets in the way, like an old password that keeps coming back. So that's the journey: in through the senses, through working memory, into long-term memory, and back out again.

Assumptions about learners

Learners are active

They select, organize, and connect information.

Learners have limited capacity

Attention and working memory can be overloaded.

Prior knowledge matters

What learners already know shapes what they notice and remember.

Learners can learn strategies

Rehearsal, chunking, and mental pictures can be taught.

Metacognition grows

Knowing how your own memory works develops with age, experience, and teaching.

Learners need feedback

It tells them if they're right and how to fix mistakes.

Let me show you what this theory assumes about learners with a little story. This boy is building a car. He picks the bricks he wants, sorts them, and snaps them together. That's the first assumption: learners are active. They select, organize, and connect information. [CLICK: limited capacity] Then he looks over and sees what his dad built: a whole house. Look at his face. It's too much at once. That's the second assumption: learners have limited capacity. Attention and working memory can be overloaded, and when that happens, learning stops. [CLICK: prior knowledge] But then he notices something. The house uses the same bricks as his car: the red bricks, the window, the yellow piece. That's the third assumption: prior knowledge matters. What learners already know shapes what they notice and remember. [CLICK: strategies] Now his dad teaches him. He shows him the picture in the instructions, they build in small parts, and they repeat the same steps. Those are strategies: mental pictures, chunking, and rehearsal. The fourth assumption is that learners can learn strategies. [CLICK: metacognition] Time passes, and he grows up a little. Now he builds the house on his own, and he plans and checks his own steps. Knowing how your own learning and memory work is called metacognition. Young children have very little of it, and it grows with age, experience, and teaching. [CLICK: feedback] Last, feedback. His dad points out one brick that's crooked, he fixes it, and then he gets a thumbs up. Driscoll explains that feedback does two jobs: it tells learners whether they're right, and it gives them information to fix their mistakes.

Instructional principles

Gain and direct attention

Why: attention is the gate to memory.

How: bold text, color, arrows, questions, surprises.

Chunk the content

Why: working memory is small.

How: small pieces, one idea at a time.

Connect to prior knowledge

Why: new ideas stick to what we know.

How: examples, stories, and analogies.

Use words and pictures

Why: two codes, two ways to recall.

How: diagrams with words (dual coding).

Give practice and feedback

Why: practice makes retrieval easier.

How: varied practice, quick feedback.

Teach memory strategies

Why: memory skills can be learned.

How: chunking, mnemonics, self‑checks.

If that's how the mind works, how should we teach? This theory gives us six clear principles. First, gain and direct attention. Driscoll suggests simple tools like bold or italic text and color, to point learners to what matters. Questions and surprises work too. [CLICK] Second, chunk the content. Working memory is small, so give learners small pieces, one idea at a time. [CLICK] Third, connect new information to what learners already know. Examples, stories, and analogies give new ideas something to attach to in long-term memory. [CLICK] Fourth, use words and pictures together. A diagram next to an explanation gives the brain two ways to store the same idea. [CLICK] Fifth, give practice and feedback, in different contexts. Practicing in different situations helps learners use what they learned in real life. [CLICK] And sixth, teach memory strategies, so learners can manage their own learning. You might notice I'm using these principles tonight. Each slide has one main idea, I use color to highlight key words, and I started with a story you could connect to.

Other theories’ views

Behaviorism

Focus on what we can see. Learning is a change in behavior, shaped by its consequences. Hidden mental steps aren’t needed to explain it.

Constructivism

Knowledge is built, not stored. Learners make their own meaning through experience and talking with others, not by copying information into memory.

Situated cognition

Learning lives in context. Knowing is tied to real activities, tools, and communities, not only to processes inside one person’s head.

Connectionism

Memory is a network, not boxes. Information is processed in parallel across many connections, instead of moving step by step through stages.

Based on Driscoll (2004).

Not everyone agrees with this theory. Behaviorists say we don't need to talk about the mind at all: learning is a change in behavior, shaped by its consequences. Constructivists say knowledge isn't simply stored like data in a computer. Learners build their own meaning, often with other people. Situated cognition says learning is tied to real situations and communities, not just to what happens inside one person's head. And connectionism, which comes from inside cognitive psychology, says memory works more like a network than a set of boxes, with many things processed at the same time.

Comparing theories

Cognitive Information Processing Behaviorism Constructivism
Learning is Taking in, processing, and storing information A change in behavior we can observe Building meaning from experience
The learner Active, with limited attention and memory Responds to the environment and its consequences Builds knowledge, often with others
The teacher Organizes information to fit how memory works Gives practice and reinforces correct responses Sets up real problems and guides inquiry
Strategies Chunking, attention cues, words with pictures Drill and practice, rewards, quick feedback Projects, case studies, group discussion

Based on Driscoll (2004).

Here's how this theory compares with two others. In Cognitive Information Processing, learning means taking in, processing, and storing information. In behaviorism, it's a change in behavior we can see. In constructivism, it's building meaning from experience. The learner is different in each: an active processor with limits, a responder to the environment, or a builder of knowledge. So the teacher's job changes too: organize information to fit memory, reinforce correct responses, or set up real problems and guide inquiry.
